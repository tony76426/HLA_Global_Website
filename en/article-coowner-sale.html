Sale of an entire property versus a co-owner's share

A tenant-in-common may generally dispose of their own fractional interest, but selling the entire jointly owned land or building involves additional legal requirements.

A buyer of a share does not automatically acquire exclusive use of a particular floor. Read Civil Code Article 819 alongside Land Act Article 34-1 and check the title records, inheritance, agency authority, restrictions and actual transaction documents.

Check both headcount and ownership percentages

For certain acts under Land Act Article 34-1, consent generally requires both more than half the co-owners and more than half the total shares.

Where consenting shares exceed two-thirds, headcount need not be counted. 'More than' is not the same as 'at least': exactly one-half or two-thirds can give a different outcome. Prepare a current share register and verify deceased owners and representatives.

Majority consent does not authorise every transaction

Management, sale, partition, gift and other acts do not necessarily follow the same majority rule. Implementing regulations limit the scope of Land Act Article 34-1; relabelling a transaction does not avoid a required consent.

Even when the numerical threshold is met, the actual price, notification and minority owners' interests matter. Do not use sham sales, concealed rebates or fictitious prices.

Give adequate prior notice

Under the applicable rules, written notice must generally be given to other co-owners before disposition, with statutory public notice where written service is impossible. Keep the notice and evidence of delivery; the recipient should understand the property, transaction and terms.

A vague group message or a photograph of one contract page may not suffice. Verify addresses, overseas owners, missing heirs and material later changes.

Pre-emption requires matching transaction terms

A co-owner may have a statutory pre-emption right when another co-owner sells a fractional share. It is not an option to set an arbitrary lower price, nor is every joint-property transaction governed identically.

Verify the property being sold, genuine price, all sale conditions, the applicable deadline and how the right is exercised. Merely opposing the sale may differ from a valid intention to purchase.

Protect the price payable to dissenting owners

A disposition by the requisite majority may trigger statutory payment or deposit requirements for non-consenting owners and supporting documentation for title transfer. Their proceeds cannot simply be held indefinitely in a seller's private account.

Record price, taxes, brokerage costs and deductions separately. Buyers should verify authority, notices and proof of payment or deposit rather than relying on a seller's assurances.

Preserve documents and deadlines when objecting

If there is a dispute about consent ratios, notice, price or pre-emption, preserve current title extracts, contracts, service evidence and dates received. Seek prompt case-specific advice on objections, protection or court proceedings.

Ignoring letters is not a reliable strategy. Alternatives to sale can include negotiated buyouts, sale of a share or partition. List the ownership type, ratio, notice, price and deadline first.

Documents for consultation

Prepare current title and encumbrance extracts, a co-owner/share calculation, the complete proposed sale contract, notices and delivery records, and documents on price payment, deposit or financing. Preserve originals and identify missing items.

Frequently asked questions

Does consent from more than two-thirds of the shares remove the notice duty?

No; the voting threshold and notices/payment protections are separate.

Can a pre-empting co-owner insist on a lower price?

Not at will: the applicable statute and the same price and transaction conditions must be checked.
