Jurisdiction and Governing Law in Cross-Border Contracts

Background: One contract, several jurisdictions

A Taiwan company and foreign supplier may negotiate, sign, ship, pay and hold assets in different countries. A clause choosing one country's law does not automatically decide the forum, evidence rules or enforceability elsewhere. Map the contracting parties, assets, performance locations and evidence before a dispute arises.

Related reading: Organizing Transaction Documents and Milestones

Separate governing law, forum and enforcement

Governing law identifies the legal rules; jurisdiction or arbitration clauses identify where disputes are heard.

Service, translation and evidentiary forms affect proceedings, while recognition and execution depend on the location of assets. Map currencies, languages and time zones as well: they may not determine jurisdiction, but affect notices, proof and coordination.

Check foreign elements and applicable rules

Taiwan's Act Governing the Choice of Law in Civil Matters Involving Foreign Elements supplies an important framework, with civil procedure and other rules for dispute proceedings. The effectiveness of choice-of-law, court or arbitration provisions depends on wording, the legal relationship and the jurisdictions concerned. Foreign mandatory law and procedures should be checked with qualified local professionals where needed.

Short clauses can conceal major problems

A template may name a court without stating exclusivity, non-contractual claims, notice addresses, language or document requirements. Different group companies may sign, pay and receive goods. Emails and amendment versions across time zones add authority and date questions. Preserve the actual parties, contracts and communications rather than assuming one short clause resolves everything.

Place clauses back in the transaction

Before signing, list entities, signature authority, payment and delivery points, accessible assets, document languages and witnesses. Compare forum choices against likely enforcement needs, costs and business duration. After dispute, inspect the clause, actual performance, proof, opponent's assets and immediate deadlines. Allocate clear roles to local and overseas counsel using one verified factual record.

Do not treat foreign law as a file attachment

Foreign law changes and requires jurisdiction-specific verification; translated summaries or old templates are not authoritative substitutes. Translate definitions, figures, annexes and formalities accurately.

Cross-border transfer of personal data, trade secrets or regulated information needs separate protection. Verify confidentiality and version control with translation platforms and local advisers, stating assumptions and scope of advice.

Prepare a cross-border relationship chart

Bring every signed version, annex, transaction flow, invoices, payments, shipping or service evidence, communications, notice addresses, company details and possible asset locations. Record each participant's country, language, dates and contact. Keep originals of any court or arbitration papers, including when and how they were served; response periods may be short.

Example: Two documents name different courts

Suppose a master contract names one court, later purchase-order terms name another, and emails expand the services. Determine which documents form the contract, whether terms were validly incorporated, signatory authority and later performance before choosing the relevant clause. A favourable sentence cannot be isolated from the document hierarchy; this example does not prejudge validity.

Work backwards from a usable result

During contracting, ask where a remedy could be sought and which assets could satisfy it. Once a dispute exists, check deadlines, notice, evidence and preservation needs first.

Then compare costs, language, timing and enforcement routes. Account for holidays, translation, notarisation and service across jurisdictions. A shared fact file and clear responsibility help avoid a paper victory that cannot be enforced.
