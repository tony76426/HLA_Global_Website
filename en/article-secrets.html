Background: Not every internal file is a trade secret

Large downloads before resignation may be concerning, but the contents, protections, business purpose and subsequent use all require verification. Not every workplace document has the same legal status. Employees may not know what must be returned or retained. Unclear classification and access control make it harder to establish the rights later, even with a confidentiality clause.

Investigate both the information and the conduct

For the information, check its origin, commercial value, public availability and protection. For conduct, check which user accessed, downloaded, sent or used what data, when, with which account and permission.

Compare logs against job duties and agreements. A traffic alert alone proves neither illegality nor safety. Prioritise genuinely valuable, hard-to-obtain information with workable protections.

Trade-secret and employment rules intersect

Taiwan's Trade Secrets Act protects qualifying information and regulates relevant conduct; labour law and contracts also affect employment and departure. Whether protection exists and an act exceeded authority depends on the facts and measures taken. Non-competes, personal data and device-use terms require their own tests. Ordinary professional experience is not necessarily protected confidential information.

Shared folders and personal devices create ambiguity

Shared logins and syncing files to private devices can obscure who accessed which version. Customer lists may exist in CRM, spreadsheets and contacts with different content and restrictions. Indiscriminate inspection of personal devices raises privacy and authority issues. Define the systems and legally obtainable logs before investigating, rather than collecting everything without a clear scope.

Protect information before employees leave

Classify sensitive data, set permissions, keep access/version logs and explain safeguards to staff. Offboarding should list accounts, devices, documents, customer contacts and unfinished tasks, with an acknowledged handover date. When an anomaly appears, preserve system evidence first and conduct focused interviews. A workable preventive process is more useful than a blanket prohibition issued afterwards.

Investigations need privacy and operational limits

Limit access to sealed logs and record who collected and reviewed them. Do not have colleagues secretly log into other people's accounts or image all personal devices without legal review.

Preserve business continuity when restricting access. Check contractors' lingering accounts and post-termination return or deletion duties. Verify the law and evidence before accusing a departing employee publicly.

Start consultation with a specific dataset

Choose the records most at issue and bring their contents, versions, creation and modification history, access rules, secrecy and employment agreements, logs, handover lists and known external destinations. Note what is already public and who normally uses the material. A focused sample can reveal legal requirements and urgent business risk without handing over an entire company drive.

Example: A customer list emailed privately

An employee sends a customer list to a private mailbox before leaving and says it was for handover. Preserve the transmission, contents, duties and handover instructions, then verify permission and alternatives. Whether the list qualifies as a trade secret, contains personal data or was later used are distinct questions. The private address alone does not establish every element of liability.

Stop damage first, then decide legal claims

Preserve lawfully available logs, secure accounts and customer service, then review the dataset, authority and conduct in stages. Compare notices, negotiation and legal procedures after assessing deadlines.

Long-term fixes include clearer classification, fewer shared accounts and verified offboarding. Real protection is demonstrable information management, not a more emphatic confidentiality slogan.
