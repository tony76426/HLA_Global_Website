Background: Risk accumulates during performance

Negotiators often focus on price, volume and delivery while leaving remedies vague. When supply, funding or market conditions change, messages become distorted and small problems grow. Risk allocation should specify who reports deviations, when decisions are required and which workable remedies exist, rather than presuming which party will be at fault.

Rank responsibilities and response options

Consider delay, defects, erroneous data, third-party claims and payment difficulties. For each ask who must inform and act, when the other side learned of the issue, whether cure is possible and how loss is documented. A blanket liability cap cannot replace transaction-specific reporting and remedy procedures; each event may require different deadlines.

Contract remedies and statutory law work together

Taiwan Civil Code rules on performance, breach and damages provide a framework alongside terms of individual deals and any special statutes. Notice, cure, termination and risk-sharing provisions must be assessed against the actual contract and performance. Limitation periods, forum, evidence and preservation may alter strategy. No provision can be declared valid or invalid in the abstract.

Common dispute: Knowing a problem without acting

A sales employee may hear of delay while the responsible manager is never notified. A quality issue may be reported without retaining samples or testing conditions. A promised cure may lack a completion date. Preserve the original notice and separate factual confirmation, proposed remedy and reservation of rights instead of relying on later recollections.

Build staged responses around realistic scenarios

Use six steps: detection, notification, verification, remedy, negotiation and exit. Assign a person, deadline and evidence requirement to each, with escalation for serious incidents. During an actual dispute, compare continued performance, contract revision, termination or proceedings against commercial goals, legal deadlines and practical enforcement prospects.

Preserve originals and control distribution

Keep contracts, system logs, tests and inspection records with appropriate access restrictions. Mark unverified points in internal summaries so they do not become repeated as facts. For urgent asset transfers or expiring periods, seek individual legal review quickly. External communications should be recorded and avoid overly certain promises when essential information is still missing.

Prepare risks as concrete questions

Bring the contract and annexes, timeline, delivery or payment evidence, test results, notices, internal responses and estimated losses. State the outcomes to avoid, acceptable remedies, whether the relationship must continue and urgent dates. Even with incomplete information, identify where missing records can be found so counsel can separate immediate action from negotiable matters.

Example: Delivery delay and broken information flow

A supplier warns purchasing of possible material delay, but project and customer teams are not told. Weeks later delivery fails and the parties dispute when the risk was known and whether loss could have been reduced. Compare supplier notices, internal forwarding, alternatives and client responses. These records can assess risk management even before final responsibility is decided.

Prevent problems at controllable checkpoints

Prioritise visible milestones: progress reports, key material confirmation, quality sampling and payment anomalies. Name the reporter, escalation recipient and decision deadline.

Match contractual notices to staffed contacts and realistic cure periods. Review smaller incidents to improve actual internal procedures and insurance or third-party arrangements; never rewrite original event records afterwards.
