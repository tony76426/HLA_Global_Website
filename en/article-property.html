Background: One property, several descriptions

Advertisements, site views, title extracts, floor plans and contracts can describe different boundaries or features. Parking, common areas, extensions, leased space and retained equipment are often simplified during viewings. Before price negotiations become binding, compare registered rights with actual use and flag discrepancies that could otherwise surface only at handover.

Related reading: Buying Property with Parking: Rights to Review

What is being bought, and when can it be used?

Verify land and building owners, shared interests and encumbrances. Identify leases, loans of use, management rules and occupation affecting possession. Coordinate payment, title transfer, inspection and physical delivery. For special equipment or unfinished works, specify permits, promises and repair responsibility. Registered title and practical occupation must both be checked.

Related reading: When a Tenant Will Not Vacate

Review title documents and contract together

Civil Code property, sale, lease and performance provisions are relevant, alongside the Land Act and applicable planning, building and condominium regulations. Current title extracts, annexes and on-site conditions must be reviewed together. Tax or construction issues may need specialist input; no single document replaces the whole due diligence process.

Frequent disputes: Area, defects and handover

Disagreement may concern a parking bay's title, common-area use, third-party occupation, missing equipment or conditions differing from viewing promises. Parties may dispute inspection dates, repairs and withholding part of the price. Preserve viewing records, conversations, photographs, inspection reports and handover minutes. Separate established facts from matters needing expert appraisal.

A three-layer due diligence table

Layer one is title: registered rights, encumbrances and parties. Layer two is the site: possession, use, equipment and management. Layer three is the transaction: price, financing, transfer, inspection and delivery. For discrepancies decide whether to seek documents, reprice, add conditions or postpone closing. If a dispute exists, reconstruct promises and performance chronologically before selecting remedies.

Do not treat unverified matters as settled

Document important facilities, leases and occupation in annexes, stating sources and dates for further confirmation. Link payments and transfers to specified documents; obtain signed inspection and handover records. Check third-party loan and administrative timetables early. An informal 'it should be fine' in a chat is not a substitute for verifying contested conditions.

Consultation requires rights and site materials

Bring land/building extracts, plans, condominium rules, leases or use agreements, contracts and annexes, payment and loan plans, viewing or inspection notes, photos and key communications. Mark any common ownership, parking, extensions or works, the desired use date, non-negotiable requirements and outstanding checks.

Example: A disputed parking space

A purchaser used a parking place during viewing, but the contract annex lists only building details and not the bay's title or use. When another person claims it after closing, oral statements may be insufficient. Check title, plans, covenants, use agreements, advertisements and discussions, then determine what the seller actually promised and could deliver.

Resolve information gaps before closing

Before signing, list matters that titles and inspection cannot answer. Before settlement, recheck encumbrances, price and document conditions; arrange photos, an equipment inventory and signed handover.

Where a gap cannot be closed, negotiate a condition, retention of funds or delay rather than vague assurances. Preserve pre- and post-transaction records and seek documented answers from sellers and brokers.
