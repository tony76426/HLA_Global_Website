Inheritance Planning: Documents to Gather First

Background: Establish people and facts first

Bereavement, care responsibilities and financial pressure often arise together. Some relatives know the assets; others know the care history or live overseas. Negotiating shares before confirming the heirs and property inventory mixes facts with expectations. Create a common record showing what documents support, what still needs investigation and what personal needs should be discussed.

Core issues: Status, assets and prior arrangements

Identify each person's legal status, assets, debts and encumbrances, then check wills, gifts, insurance and arrangements made during life. A family's description of an item, or who holds its documents, does not decide ownership. Joint property, shares in businesses and overseas assets may involve additional rules. Confirm applicable rights, deadlines and procedures individually.

Related reading: Inherited Debts: Liability, Renunciation and Inventory · Wills and Reserved Shares under Taiwan Law

Current Civil Code rules are the starting point

Taiwan's succession rules, property rules and debt provisions provide the framework. Family relationships, asset types and existing instruments affect the order of analysis. Tax, trust, insurance, corporate and cross-border issues may require other specialists. Some choices are time-limited or formally prescribed; generic online answers are no substitute for checking specific rights, liabilities and document validity.

Frequent disputes: Unequal information and expectations

Some heirs control banking and land records while others see fragments; oral wishes may differ from formal documents; long-term carers may feel overlooked. These are legal as well as relational issues. Accusing someone of concealment or misappropriation before investigating may obstruct cooperation. List confirmed facts, unresolved questions and proposals separately, preserving original records.

Counsel's approach: Legal and practical inventories

Track two lines in parallel. The legal line covers heirs, assets, debts, documents and deadlines. The practical line covers housing, care, business continuity and immediate funds. Compare keeping joint ownership, transfers, sales and alternatives only after both lines are clear. Counsel can organise fact gathering, procedural steps and focused negotiations; serious disagreements may require a separate evidence analysis.

Preserve documents and communicate carefully

Secure identity, property and liability records before anyone changes accounts or disposes of disputed assets. Family meetings should distinguish mutually verified points from open questions without turning every note into an accusation. Older people, minors and those with special care needs require practical attention. Filing, form and limitation requirements vary, so seek individual advice rather than relying solely on this public article.

A common starting file for consultation

Collect family registers, identity papers, asset and debt schedules, property extracts, bank and investment records, business holdings, insurance, wills, gifts and key communications. If items are missing, identify who may hold them. Note each person's housing, care and business needs and the outcomes they hope to avoid. The purpose is to identify what must be verified urgently, not choose shares immediately.

Example: A family business and the home

Suppose the estate includes operating-company shares and a home occupied by relatives. Some heirs favour sale while others want to retain the enterprise and residence. Equal monetary shares may overlook management, cash flow and care. First verify owners, company documents, debts, occupancy and needs; then consider immediate arrangements and long-term distribution separately. No single allocation follows automatically.

Priorities: Protect evidence and deadlines

First address urgent legal and tax dates and preserve identity and asset records. Second, create a shared list distinguishing verified and uncertain information.

Only then compare division, management or disposition. Agree on information-sharing contacts; where trust has broken down, independent advice may be needed. Family meetings can begin with neutral document exchange rather than demanding immediate consent, reducing the risk that silence is mistaken for agreement.
