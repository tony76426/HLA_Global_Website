Background: Participation is not authority

Major purchases, investments and asset sales may pass through departments, supervisors, boards and signatories. Everyone may have participated, yet their decision-making powers differ. Without clear records, changes of staff or later challenges make the original basis hard to trace. Governance documentation should show who decided what, under which authority and information, not create empty formalities.

Who decides, represents and executes?

Separate internal authority from external representation or agency. Check limits, required meetings, disclosure and conflicts of interest, then verify that execution matches the approved terms. Approval of proposal A does not necessarily authorise signing version B. Company type, articles of association, roles and transaction size matter more than a person's title alone.

Read statutes, articles and delegations together

Company Act rules supply the organisation and resolution framework; civil-law agency and contract rules affect external acts. Compare the articles, rules of procedure, delegation matrices, appointment documents and minutes. Customary practice cannot waive mandatory approvals. Review the versions in force at the relevant time and additional sector-specific rules when needed.

Documents may exist yet contradict one another

Minutes may say only 'approved', without a price or limits; attachments may change without reapproval; a monetary cap may be bypassed by splitting documents; or related parties may not disclose interests fully. A late summary is not a substitute for original materials. Preserve each version with when, by whom and to which decision-maker information was supplied.

Design governance for actual workflows

Start with a short checklist for high-risk matters: purpose, options, risks, participants, authority and execution owner. Give meeting participants the same briefing package; link resolutions to the signed version and require reporting of changes. A usable process beats excessive paperwork. If established practice conflicts with the articles, determine whether procedures or formal rules need revision.

Review delegations as organisations change

Restructuring, new investors, capital raising and market entry may make old mandates obsolete. Date each version and identify current and substitute approvers; remove departed staff from active flows. For e-signatures, remote meetings and cloud records, check identity, integrity and storage. Separate confidential internal approval materials from documents disclosed externally.

Documents to reconstruct a decision

Prepare articles, board/shareholder rules, authority matrices, notices, agendas, proposals, minutes, signed versions, implementation reports and personnel changes. For investments or related-party matters include interests and disclosure history. Ask not only whether a resolution passed, but which information was available, what was authorised and how it was implemented.

Example: Negotiations expand an approved investment

A board approves an investment ceiling and principal conditions, but negotiations add guarantees, different payment dates or related-party terms. Compare the original resolution, proposal, revised drafts and signed agreement. Identify who knew of changes and whether further approval was required. The record should capture material limits, not merely the eventual vote.

Separate past remedies from future reforms

For completed transactions, preserve contemporaneous records and assess external obligations and corrective procedures. For future matters, revise delegation thresholds, proposal formats and change reporting.

Do not combine both in backdated minutes. Pilot a concise decision table for key risks, recording considered alternatives, limits, exceptions and their approval dates so new staff can understand the decision.
