Before Product Launch: Data, Licenses and Platform Terms

Background: Product changes outpace legal terms

A new feature can introduce third-party content, analytics, external models or cross-border cloud services while existing notices remain unchanged. Designers, engineers, marketers and legal staff may have different views of where data goes. A launch review should begin with what users do, what data the system collects, who receives it and how outputs are used—not merely how many policy documents appear on the website.

Key issues: Rights, permissions and data flows

Identify the origin and permitted use of code, designs, text, images and datasets, including employee and contractor arrangements. Diagram the collection, use, storage, disclosure and deletion of personal data. Product commitments must match real functions. With generative tools, assess confidential inputs, human verification of outputs and third-party licence conditions.

Related reading: Generative AI: Checking Source Materials and Licensing · Employee Departures and Trade Secrets

Legal assessment depends on the type of information and result

Taiwan's Copyright Act, Personal Data Protection Act and contract rules govern different aspects of the product. Non-personal information may still be confidential, while downloadable material may not be licensed for commercial use or sublicensing. The actual source, purpose, data flow, contract terms and service locations control the assessment. Recheck the current law and platform policies before launch.

Common disputes: Policies and product behaviour diverge

A privacy notice may promise service-only use while analytics share data elsewhere; a contractor agreement may omit ownership or modification rights; image and font sources may not be traceable; and deleting an account may not explain what happens to retained data. Unchecked generated content may infringe third-party rights or repeat errors. A longer disclaimer cannot repair mismatched workflows and records.

Build a maintainable product-and-data register

For each feature, record data sources, lawful basis, user notice, third-party recipients, retention and a responsible owner. Review legal terms alongside the interface, adding approval checkpoints for new features and suppliers. Prioritise high-impact data, irreversible sharing and materials of uncertain origin. A common diagram allows product and legal teams to translate general rules into actionable changes.

Keep evidence of technical decisions

Retain licences, contractor agreements, data-flow diagrams, system settings and dated policy versions. Set confidentiality, access and human-review rules for generative-AI inputs and outputs; automation does not guarantee factual or legal accuracy. Reassess cross-border suppliers and changes in law or platform terms periodically. This overview is not a compliance certification for any particular product.

Documents for a product-and-law review

Prepare function diagrams or screenshots, code and material source lists, supplier and licence agreements, data fields and flows, third-party services, privacy notices, terms of service and version history. Identify upcoming features, users, operating jurisdictions and priority risks. A narrative description of data entering and leaving the system is enough to begin if a formal flowchart does not yet exist.

Example: Adding an external analytics service

Suppose engineers connect a third-party analytics tool and marketing begins using the output, but user notices and supplier lists still describe the old system. Identify transmitted fields, purposes, recipients, storage locations and retention periods; compare them with the existing interface and commitments. Personal data and cross-border processing may trigger additional statutory or contractual checks. Review should accompany the change, not follow it.

Prioritise high-impact data flows

Address sensitive data, hard-to-reverse sharing, unlicensed materials and unreviewed suppliers first; restrict risky uses while permissions, notices and safeguards are settled.

Use reusable checklists for lower-risk functions and record launch versions and reasons. Users should understand what their data does, who receives it and how to request changes without needing an engineer's oral explanation.
