Background: One parking space, different descriptions

A salesperson may promise a fixed bay, handover papers may assign a number, and the condominium later invokes rotation under its rules. The purchaser may believe a separately transferable right was acquired, while the seller or committee sees shared use. Check registration, contract, sales statements and actual occupation rather than assuming a painted bay number proves title.

What was acquired, and who controls use?

Identify the registered right, co-ownership or use agreement; compare the sale documents for location, area, price and transfer provisions. Then inspect community rules, owners' meeting resolutions and actual allocation.

Claims against the seller about delivery differ from claims against the management body about common facilities. Ask how the parking right follows the apartment on resale.

Property, contract and condominium rules intersect

Civil-law property and contract rules, the Condominium Administration Act and community covenants may all affect rights. Applicability and binding effect on successors depend on title documents and transaction timing. Advertisements may inform contract interpretation but a single image rarely settles title. Compare registry and approved drawings with all annexes.

Common conflicts: Plans, numbers and site differ

A contract may promise one space without identifying it, later marking changes may alter numbers, and title, permits or management lists may use different labels. Duplicate promises and mechanical-space dimensions can also matter. Preserve original advertisements, signed plans, handover records, photographs and notices, noting each document's date and source.

Use four columns to compare the sources

Make a table for registration and drawings, contract and annexes, sales communications, and community management and current use. Record the described bay, date, transferability and contradictions in each column. If multiple residents are involved, separate each buyer's sale rights from common management questions before identifying the proper respondent and interim arrangements.

Do not rely on oral sales promises alone

Look for contemporaneous advertisements, messages, emails, meeting records and payment proof when oral promises differ from documents. New-build and resale rights may not transfer identically.

Do not occupy another bay or redraw markings yourself. Request the covenant and owners' resolutions in force at purchase as well as later versions, rather than assuming one recent notice explains everything.

Reconstruct the path from purchase to today

Bring the sale agreement, parking annex, building title, plans, permits, advertisements, payments, handover papers and management rules. Record move-in, actual bay, any swaps, dates of notices and immediate parking needs. Mark documents held by the developer, broker or committee; their legal effect need not be assumed before consultation.

Example: A fixed bay becomes rotational

A buyer receives a plan marking B2-18 and uses that space for three years; the community later introduces rotation. Review how the plan became part of the contract, rules at purchase, management authority, historical use and adoption of the new resolution. Neither three years' use nor a newer covenant alone determines the whole legal position.

Priorities: Stabilise use, then allocate responsibility

Where a bay is vital, consider temporary arrangements and evidence preservation first. Then identify rights, responsible parties and negotiations; finally compare performance, alternative use, price adjustment or damages.

Specify the desired result, such as keeping the bay or an equivalent replacement. Consider planned resale, leasing and financing, because unclear rights may create later transaction issues.
