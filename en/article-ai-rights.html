Generative AI: Checking Source Materials and Licensing

Background: More tools, less clarity about sources

A design team may combine client photographs, existing brand copy, licensed stock images and internal drafts in a generative-AI tool, then revise the output by hand. By delivery, the final picture no longer reveals which materials were lawfully supplied, which portions were created by people or which third-party licence terms still apply. The issue is not simply whether AI can be used, but whether every source and stage of use can be justified.

Key issues: Rights from input through delivery

Identify who owns each input, the licence scope, personal-data obligations and confidentiality limits. Review platform terms, generated output, human selection and revisions, then check commercial-use permissions, sublicensing, warranties and the client agreement.

Similarities to third-party works and ownership claims require case-specific review; a download button is no legal clearance. Businesses should also distinguish approved enterprise accounts from employees' personal subscriptions, because retention and access settings differ.

Legal framework: Copyright and data protection

Copyright rules apply to protected material and acts of exploitation; Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act concerns the collection and use of identifiable personal data. Trade secrets, likeness rights, trademarks and contractual restrictions may add further obligations. As tools and outputs evolve, ownership cannot be resolved by a single blanket rule. Assess the actual material, human contribution, platform terms and intended use under current law.

Common disputes after a client delivery

A stock-image licence may permit certain displays but not model input; an employee may upload unreleased product information; or a contractor's private account may prevent retrieval of prompts and edits. Sometimes a delivered design resembles another person's work. Without a production record, explaining or correcting such problems is difficult. Recording source licences and review decisions early reduces the cost of later remedial action.

Counsel's approach: Map materials and decisions

Map seven stages: sourcing, input, generation, human selection, editing, review and delivery. For each, record the person, tool, version, audience and presence of client or personal information. Promises to clients must match verified procedures; do not warrant an absolute absence of third-party risks without checking. Apply more intensive review to sensitive content, large-scale publication and public-facing campaigns.

Practical precautions for sensitive material

Use non-confidential, properly authorised and de-identified materials during trials. Expand use only after reviewing system settings, contracts, permissions and retention policies.

The absence of source text in the output does not eliminate input-data risk. Adopt clear rules on prohibited uploads, source checks, commercial approval and human-edit records. Prioritise intensive checks for identifiable people and large campaigns, while keeping lighter records for low-risk internal drafts.

Documents to prepare for an initial review

Gather the delivered version, original materials, licences, platform or procurement terms, account and access settings, prompt/edit history, delivery obligations and planned publication channels. Confidential content need not be sent casually: describe its category first and arrange secure review. Distinguish verified rights, uncertain sources and materials not yet used rather than labelling an entire AI project lawful or unlawful at once.

Example: Using a client's internal photographs

Suppose a team uploads a client's internal event photograph to an outside tool, generates a campaign visual and later makes extensive manual revisions. Before release, review the people and background objects pictured, permitted data use, tool terms, generated and revised versions and the client's publication rights. If clearance remains uncertain, substitute assets or recreate the design. The illustration does not allege infringement by any platform.

Priorities: Control inputs, then outputs

First list information that must never be uploaded and restrict access. Second, apply different review standards to drafts and commercial products.

Third, embed source, edit and delivery records into the workflow. If a published work raises concerns, preserve versions, limit unnecessary circulation and consider replacement and communication. Update authorisation records whenever a design changes: an old licence may not cover newly added material.
