Exclusive Use of Co-Owned Property

Key Takeaways

  • Distinguish shares from actual occupation
  • Management and preservation follow different rules
  • Compensation for exclusive use needs a legal basis

Distinguish shares from actual occupation

Civil Code Article 818 addresses a co-owner's use and benefit of common property, but a fractional share is not a specified room. Long-term residence or holding keys does not prove an exclusive right.

Confirm ownership type, partition-of-use agreements and management decisions. Oral family arrangements may be relevant; assess messages, minutes, leases and later changes rather than assuming only written agreements count.

Management and preservation follow different rules

Civil Code Article 820 deals with management and possible judicial adjustment; necessary preservation or simple repairs can differ from changing the whole building's use. List each proposed act, why it is needed, required consent and expense sharing.

Unequal management or changed circumstances may warrant court review. A large share does not justify excluding others or withholding every common expense.

Compensation for exclusive use needs a legal basis

If one co-owner occupies beyond agreed limits and excludes others, unjust enrichment or another claim may arise, but valid consent may change the result.

Sole residence is not an automatic right to charge full market rent for every past month. Establish the period, area, notices, fair rental value, co-ownership shares and any offsetting contributions or prior permission.

Account for rent and expenses separately

For outside leases, verify who signed, their authority, rent received and deposit held. Record income, taxes, management, repairs and advances item by item rather than only a net figure.

Civil Code Article 822 concerns common expenses, subject to agreements and facts. Necessary maintenance, major improvements and private decoration differ. Receipts should show dates, purposes and payers.

Keep neutral records even without cooperation

Request specific documents and discussions in writing, such as leases, rent accounts, repairs and a new use plan. Give clear deadlines and preserve full messages.

Avoid changing locks, utilities, possessions or entering a dwelling by force simply because you own a share. For urgent threats seek appropriate assistance; for ordinary disputes assess management, compensation or legal proceedings.

Resolve current use before ending co-ownership

Options include fixed-term use, monthly compensation, shared rent accounting, repair authority or a buyout. Specify payment calculations, dates and review points; temporary permission is not permanent waiver.

If management cannot continue, consider partition or sale separately. Old expense and occupation claims do not vanish automatically through partition. Compare the users' living needs and the economics of exit.

Prepare a record that reconstructs the history

Bring title extracts, how ownership was acquired, old agreements, rent and expense records, notices, photographs and management history. Clarify the desired outcome: information, restored use, distributed rent, compensation or an end to co-ownership.

Different remedies require different legal grounds and evidence. A neutral chronology helps keep legal issues separate from family emotions.

Documents for consultation

Prepare title extracts, acquisition grounds, co-use and management agreements, leases, rent and deposits, repair and advance receipts, notices and the timeline of occupation and contacts.

Frequently asked questions

May I charge rent whenever another co-owner lives there?

Not automatically: use agreements and the applicable compensation rules matter.

Can I change the locks because I have a share?

No automatic right exists to ignore occupants' possession, home and safety.
