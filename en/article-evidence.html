Background: Evidence resolves conflicting accounts

One side says delivery occurred; the other denies acceptance. One says a deadline was extended; the other relies on the original date. Signed documents, attachments, transmission records, messages and actual receipt matter more than 'everyone knew'. As a dispute begins, stop the loss of records and separate originals from later summaries.

Separate the claim, supporting facts and proof

For payment, identify the obligation, calculation, maturity and performance already made; for damages, document loss and causation. Include foreseeable defences.

Mark each record as an original, copy or summary and note who holds other versions. Evidence should present the entire course of performance, not merely favourable fragments.

Contract law and procedure both matter

Civil Code rules on obligations, breach and damages are starting points, but the contract and facts determine particular rights. Litigation has its own pleading and proof requirements; arbitration, mediation or special procedures may alter timing. No webpage can determine an item's evidentiary value in isolation. Counsel must consider authenticity, lawful acquisition, completeness and relevance.

Screenshots often lose context

A chat image may omit the date, participants and surrounding messages. Cloud documents with the same name may have different contents; a photo may lack location or timing. A payment record without its contractual link may prove little about performance. Assign each item a number, source and capture date and retain native files and backups instead of relying solely on a polished presentation.

Use a mutually checkable chronology

A five-column table can record date, event, your evidence, the other side's evidence and outstanding questions. Put one event per row, linking contract clauses and document names. Mark uncertainty explicitly, and compare payments, delivery, objections, demands and replies. This common factual baseline helps negotiation and case preparation without substituting for legal analysis.

Preservation does not justify illegal collection

Protect personal data, trade secrets and access privileges. Do not enter unauthorised accounts, alter file timestamps or delete inconvenient versions.

Where systems overwrite data, arrange lawful preservation promptly with IT staff and record what was done. Review auto-deletion and backup policies and restrict copying to authorised people. Integrity matters more than volume.

Prepare originals and a missing-document list

Bring contracts, attachments, amendments, quotations and specifications, payments, delivery and acceptance records, objections, communications, formal demands and imminent deadlines. Record where originals are held and who may possess missing evidence. The first consultation should identify evidentiary gaps before rushing to draft a pleading.

Example: Delayed delivery and an oral extension

A supplier was due to deliver by month-end, later discussed a two-week extension by telephone, while the buyer emailed revised specifications and partial goods arrived. Compare the follow-up emails, invoices, shipping and acceptance records and specification versions. Neither a demand letter nor an oral recollection alone establishes whether the deadline changed.

Plan preservation and negotiation together

Check limitation, notice and asset risks promptly, while considering settlement. Identify commercial interests to protect, acceptable cures and unacceptable costs; compare strength of proof, enforceability and time.

Assign owners and due dates to next steps. Add new negotiation records to the same chronology, and specify performance, payment and notification terms if an agreement is reached.
